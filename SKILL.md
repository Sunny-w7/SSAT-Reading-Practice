# SSAT Reading Practice and Assessment

Use English for passages, questions, teaching notes, and feedback. Treat the procedures below as practice-design standards. Claims of official scoring or statistical equivalence require independent evidence.

## 1. Establish the task and materials

- Follow the requested task: select passages, write a set, grade responses, or summarize progress. A submission of answers calls for grading or review.
- Default to four passages with five questions each: fiction, science or nature, humanities argument, and poetry. Suggest 22 minutes for the first pass and five minutes for independent review. Identify this as a practice schedule. Adapt to a request for 40 questions or another length. Label the 20-question set as practice.
- Check existing questions, versions, keys, feedback, and previously read excerpts. If the passage or choices are missing, organize the response record and leave the cause of an error undetermined.
- Use official samples supplied by the user as anchors after inspecting their contents. Identify third-party materials. Verify new sources and current test specifications online.
- When continuing this series, consult the [historical calibration record](references/project-calibration.md). Use recent observations to assess current performance.

## 2. Select passages

Start with the source. For detailed screening, use the select-ssat-upper-passages skill when available.

1. Open the full text in a reliable edition, public archive, or Project Gutenberg. Record the author, work, edition, translator if applicable, chapter, URL, exact opening and closing words, and word count. Leave unknown fields blank and label estimated counts. Observe copyright and quotation limits. Check jurisdiction before claiming public-domain status.
2. Select a continuous excerpt from one edition. Preserve its internal text, spelling, and wording. Check poetry variants. Verify and disclose any typographical repairs. Switch to a reliable edition if the wording itself is doubtful.
3. Aim for roughly 250–550 English words of prose. A complete short poem may be shorter. Use natural boundaries that preserve the local argument or scene.
4. Require enough context within the excerpt to resolve references, conflict, and reasoning without outside plot, biography, scientific knowledge, or later chapters.
5. Identify at least three distinct inference targets, such as viewpoint, motive, qualification, concession, argumentative function, or the dimension of a comparison. Vary the evidence as well as the wording.
6. For each target, record the textual clue, warranted inference, plausible overreach, and decisive distinction. Select passages that let readers resolve a close final pair from the text.
7. Score local completeness, independence from background, inference density, potential for plausible distractors, language suitability, and textual fidelity from 0 to 2 each. Explain each score. Use the total out of 12 for editorial screening. Defer a passage if sourcing, independence, distinct targets, or answer uniqueness fails, regardless of total.
8. Check for duplication at excerpt level. Identify the new scene or argument when reusing a work. Account for prior exposure when interpreting scores.

## 3. Write questions and choices

- Record the evidence and intended skill first. Specify what the stem asks for: overall meaning, a word in context, inference, attitude, or structural function. Resolve ambiguity before using “best” in a stem.
- Vary the five questions. Typically cover overall understanding, local language or syntax, inference, and structural or rhetorical function. Choose the fifth focus to suit the passage.
- In science, distinguish observation, hypothesis, experimental conditions, conclusion, and scope. In fiction, track narrator judgment, character feelings, and self-awareness. In argument, examine concessions, counterexamples, and different causes of the same result. In poetry, resolve syntax, references, comparisons, and local images before interpreting theme.
- Write five text-related choices (A–E) that answer on the same dimension, use parallel grammar, and have comparable length and abstraction.
- Require the key to answer the whole stem through direct support or necessary inference. A paraphrase may be correct. Brevity and cautious wording carry no special weight.
- For each distractor, identify its textual appeal and precise defect. Vary referent, scope, degree, cause, timing, agent, condition, or certainty. Avoid deliberate vagueness and distinctions that depend only on obscure synonyms.
- Include at least one close rival that shares the central content of the key. Identify the exact word, sentence, or structural feature that rules it out. Rewrite any item that requires guessing the writer's preference.
- Resolve choices using the passage. Evaluate absolute wording against the evidence. Avoid shortcuts based on which option sounds milder.
- Check for clues from length, grammar, repeated words, and other items. Distribute key positions reasonably without a visible cycle or forced quotas.
- Keep examples, hints, and source annotations free of actual answers. Show submission syntax with placeholders: `item-choice-confidence(alternate)`.

## 4. Audit before release

Complete the [teacher item record](references/review-templates.md) for each question. Solve each item again without consulting the intended key. Build the strongest textual case for the closest rival and check whether the key adds an unsupported claim. Revise or withdraw an item with two defensible answers.

Before release, verify passage continuity and traceability, excerpt-only reasoning, one best answer, and a specific defect for every distractor. Check that inferences have evidence, overall questions cover the passage's development, and close choices turn on a meaningful reading distinction. Check for duplication, cross-item clues, and mismatches between key letters and published option order. Keep student and teacher materials separate. Freeze the version and key, and disclose the reason for any later correction.

## 5. Calibrate difficulty

Evaluate passage difficulty separately from the difficulty of distinguishing choices. Consider embedded syntax, integration across sentences, viewpoint, qualifications, and close paraphrases. Increasing length or archaic vocabulary alone is insufficient.

Compare available anchor items on reading load, inference distance, distribution of evidence, choice similarity, and time pressure. Scores and impressions alone cannot establish equivalence to an official test or support estimates of percentiles or scaled scores.

Retain some direct evidence-location questions. Target weaknesses through fresh passages while preserving breadth of genres and skills. Identify the specific dimension changed when adjusting difficulty.

## 6. Collect responses and independent review

- Interpret `1E-M(D)` as first choice E, medium confidence, alternate D. H, M, and L mean high, medium, and low confidence. An alternate becomes a final answer only through an explicit review decision.
- Accept unambiguous separator errors. Ask only about materially ambiguous entries. Keep the original submission and the normalized record.
- Preserve first-pass answers and record actual first-pass time. Withhold the key and correctness cues until independent review is complete.
- Review only items that merit another look. Allow the student to retain an answer. Record item number, retain/change decision, evidence, what the rival adds, omits, or changes, and review time.
- Ask: Who or what is the passage describing, and in what respect? Which word limits scope, condition, or degree? What does the rival add or change?
- Grade immediately if requested. Mark missing timing, reasons, or changes as “not provided.”

## 7. Grade and give feedback

- Verify the original items, version, and key. Report first-pass results separately from any submitted review decisions.
- Report number correct, denominator, and accuracy. Include sample sizes by genre, skill, and confidence. Prioritize high-confidence errors. A low-confidence correct answer still provides some evidence.
- Reassess disputed items. If uniqueness fails, withdraw the item or accept defensible answers. Show the resulting denominator and scoring impact.
- For each error, report first choice and alternate, key, exact evidence, reasoning for the key, the specific defect in the student's choice, the decisive distinction between the final two, and one action to try on a fresh item.
- Classify evidenced distinctions: meaning or syntax, referent or agent, object or dimension, scope or degree, condition or negation, causation, structure, viewpoint, added information, or integration across the passage. Treat explanations of the student's thinking as hypotheses until supported by their reasoning.
- When reporting correct alternates, also report the risk of changing a correct first choice. An alternate hit alone establishes no review gain. Confidence M alone does not warrant a change.
- With documented review, count wrong-to-right, right-to-wrong, wrong-to-different-wrong, and retained answers. Compute net gain as wrong-to-right minus right-to-wrong. Without review decisions, leave gain unreported.
- Base mastery judgments on repeated performance across passages and rounds. One correct answer, a perfect round, or an unexplained correction offers limited evidence.

## 8. Test transfer and hand off

Model the distinction with a short example. Then test the same reading relationship with fresh content, no hints, and delayed answers. After success on a local item, retest in a new full passage and a timed mixed set before judging transfer stable.

Record previously read excerpts, set versions, verified keys, first-pass and review records, observed errors, evidence, missing data, and specific next targets. If historical item text is missing, carry forward only verifiable records. Give newer observations precedence over an older snapshot.
